👑 Layer 5 · The Grandmaster's View

Why does the internet end up changing not just how information flows, but how people relate to the world?

From "information superhighway" to "reshaping how we're present"

🤔 The ordinary user: why does this question matter?

Think about a few things that shifted between 20 years ago and today:

  • Chatting with someone — used to mean a phone call or meeting up. Today, one WeChat message is enough.
  • Finding a partner — used to mean introductions or chance encounters. Today, it's an algorithm matching you.
  • Seeing the world — used to be TV news. Today, it's endless feed scrolling.
  • Expressing yourself — used to be a diary only you read. Today, a post is judged the moment you hit send.
  • Building relationships — used to require living life together. Today, you can be "cloud friends" for 10 years without meeting.

The internet was first called the "information superhighway" — but looking back, what it changed goes far beyond information.

What it really changed is "how we're present" —

how we're present in relationships, in society, in time and space.
This is the deepest shift — and the easiest one to miss.

🧩 Breaking it down

  1. The internet changed "distance" — what "near" and "far" mean is different now.
  2. The internet changed "time" — the balance between sync and async shifted.
  3. The internet changed "identity" — who are you online?
  4. The internet changed "relationships" — what counts as "knowing someone"? What counts as "close"?
  5. In the end, the internet changed "what counts as real."

🎯 Peel it back, layer by layer

The user's view: outcomes only

You feel it: "I have 1000 people in my WeChat, but only 5 I actually talk to." "I send 50 messages a day, but haven't talked to anyone face-to-face in ages." "The friends I met online understand me better than the ones in real life."

These are signals of a new kind of relationship — not good, not bad, just new.

The build view: starting to see structure

Sync vs async — the deepest shift the internet brought:

// Before: face-to-face communication mode: "synchronous" requirement: same time + same place bandwidth: extremely high (expressions, tone, smell, body) cost per interaction: high relationships: few and deep // Phone call: semi-sync mode: "voice sync" requirement: same time bandwidth: medium relationships: medium // WeChat / SMS: async mode: "asynchronous text" requirement: none bandwidth: low cost per interaction: extremely low relationships: many and shallow // Social media: broadcast async mode: "broadcast asynchronous" requirement: none bandwidth: extremely low audience: thousands to billions relationships: "following" rather than "knowing" // Every shift in communication medium // redefines "how we're together"

From "high bandwidth + high cost + few and deep" to "low bandwidth + low cost + many and shallow" — this isn't just a change in how we communicate. It's a restructuring of what a human relationship even is.

The system view: the full path

Look at how "algorithmic matching" changed intimacy:

Traditional: you meet through friends, family, coworkers, chance ↓ The relationship has "external witnesses" (everyone knows both of you) ↓ The relationship is embedded in a social network ↓ vs ↓ Today: algorithms "match" you out of millions ↓ The relationship has no "external witnesses" (just the two of you) ↓ The relationship can be "re-swiped" at any moment ↓ Intimacy becomes "commoditized": searchable, comparable, swappable

This isn't a moral judgment — just a fact: the "underlying protocol" of relationships changed. Old relationships had friction, history, witnesses. Today's relationships get abstracted into a "match score."

The trade-off view: why is it designed this way?

Every new "form of relationship" comes with its own costs and rewards:

The upside of async

Crosses time zones, always reachable, room to think before replying, friendly to introverts, boosts productivity.

The cost of async

You lose "being present together." You can't read tone. Misunderstandings multiply. 100 WeChat messages don't equal 10 minutes face-to-face.

The upside of algorithmic matching

Isolated people find each other. Niche interests gather. Geographic and class walls come down.

The cost of algorithmic matching

Choice overload → shallower commitment. Relationships feel "replaceable" → less deep investment. The "perfect match" illusion → disappointment in real relationships.

None of these costs are "the platform's conspiracy" — they're structural costs baked into the new protocol itself.

✋ Try it yourself

🔍 Observation experiment: a day without your phone

Pick a day. Turn your phone off (or put it in a drawer). After 24 hours, notice:

  1. When did you most want to grab it? Who were you looking for? For what?
  2. Without your phone that day, who did you talk to? How was it different from usual?
  3. Did "information anxiety" show up — the fear of missing something?
  4. At night, looking back, did it feel like something was missing, or like something was extra?

The point of this experiment isn't "quit your phone." It's to let you see clearly: how much of your connection to the world happens through a screen?

👑 The Grandmaster's View: how does this shape reality?

The internet changed not "information," but "how we're present"

Walk the road from Q1 to Q39 and a clearer picture emerges:

  • Q1-Q10 teach you to see "how information flows" — DNS, requests, responses, databases.
  • Q11-Q20 teach you to see "how systems are built" — architecture, caching, message queues.
  • Q21-Q30 teach you to see "how trade-offs are made" — consistency, availability, performance.
  • Q31-Q39 teach you to see "how technology shapes people" — attention, emotion, relationships.

The deepest awakening is this: the internet isn't "a new tool given to us." It's "a new condition of existence".

In the past, "being present" meant your body was there. Today, "presence" splits into layers:

  • Your body is here, your attention is on the feed.
  • You're in Shenzhen, your social life is in New York.
  • By day you're at the office, by night you're "guild leader" in a game.
  • In real life you can't get a word out; online you drop one-liners all day.

"You" are no longer a single whole — you're sliced into pieces, spread across many identities on many platforms. This is the deepest reshaping of "digital existence": we no longer just "use the internet" — we "live inside it".

So when you ask "what did I actually do today?", maybe ask first: "Today — was I present? Present to what? Present to whom?"

✨ Back to the question

So — why does the internet end up changing not just how information flows, but how people relate to the world?

The answer: because "how information flows" itself defines "how people relate to each other, how people know the world, how people know themselves." When the internet rewrote the distance, timing, density, and direction of information, it rewrote the base coordinates of human existence.

This is the ultimate meaning of "The Grandmaster's View":

You're no longer just "a user." You're an observer —
you see the system, see the design, see the process of being shaped,
and then, above all of it, you find the ability to choose again.

Once you get here, you're no longer someone shaped by the internet.
You start becoming — someone who can shape it.
