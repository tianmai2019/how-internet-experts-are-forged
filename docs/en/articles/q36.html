👑 Layer 5 · The Grandmaster's View

Why does internet product design end up shaping people's emotions, expression, and judgment?

When the technological medium rewrites the grammar of feeling

🤔 The ordinary user: why does this matter?

Have you ever felt any of these?

  • After 15 minutes of short videos, you're inexplicably irritated;
  • After a night in the comments section, you feel like picking a fight;
  • You see a news headline and you're already angry before you finish reading it;
  • You scroll past "someone else's life" in a feed and suddenly feel anxious.

These feelings aren't "you" — and they aren't the "content" either. They're a joint product of you and the product's design. The same event — read in a newspaper, heard from a friend, watched on TikTok, seen on a trending list — triggers completely different emotional reactions in you.

Product design is the "amplifier" of emotion.

It decides which feelings get turned up, which get muted,
which kinds of expression spread, and which viewpoints disappear.

🎯 Peel it back, layer by layer

The user's view: only the result

What the ordinary user feels: "This platform is so toxic." "Why are netizens so extreme?" "The internet is full of keyboard warriors."

But notice — once you close the app, that "toxicity" is gone. People didn't change. The environment did.

The implementation view: starting to see the structure

The core logic of content distribution can be expressed in a few lines of simplified pseudocode:

// Will a piece of content get recommended? Look at its "engagement rate" score = weight_1 * click_rate + weight_2 * complete_rate + weight_3 * comment_rate // comments are the priciest! + weight_4 * share_rate + weight_5 * follow_rate // What kind of content is most likely to trigger comments and shares? // —— content with strong emotion // —— content with a sharp stance // —— content that makes people angry / shocked / anxious // So the algorithm naturally "picks" this kind of content, // because it delivers the highest-value engagement

The algorithm itself has no emotional preference. But because it optimizes "engagement" — and anger is the emotion most likely to drive engagement — it systematically amplifies anger. This isn't a conspiracy. It's the natural output of the math.

The system view: seeing the full path

An "emotional positive feedback loop" plays out like this:

Angry content → high engagement → algorithm pushes it more ↓ More people see it → more people comment → more people share ↓ An angry comment section stokes fresh anger ↓ Creators notice "anger gets traffic" → produce more anger ↓ The whole platform's "emotional baseline" gets pulled up

This is exactly what that famous MIT paper said: "Falsehood diffuses faster than truth." (Rumors travel faster than the truth — because they're more surprising.)

The trade-off view: why design it this way?

You might ask — why not have the algorithm optimize for "calm, thoughtful content" instead? The answer is that if it did, the platform would lose traffic, and users would drift away:

Anger-driven design

Users stay longer, engage more, ad revenue is high — but the social mood gets torn apart.

Calm-driven design

Users are more rational, the content is better — but time-on-app is shorter, growth is slower, and commercially it can't beat the competition.

Under the pressure of commercial competition, almost every platform gets pulled toward the former. It's not one person's choice — it's the equilibrium of a whole industry.

✋ Try it yourself

🔍 The emotion journal experiment

Run a 3-day observation:

  1. Before you open a given app, record your mood (1–10);
  2. After you close it, record it again;
  3. Look at the data: which apps made you feel better? Which made you feel worse?
  4. Bonus round: pick one day where you don't open Weibo / TikTok / your feed at all, and notice what you find yourself thinking about.

A lot of people finish this experiment and get shocked — certain apps leave me more irritated every single time, but I can't stop opening them. That's "emotional engineering" made concrete.

👑 The Grandmaster's View: how does this shape reality?

The technological medium rewrites the grammar of feeling

Marshall McLuhan said: "The medium is the message." In the 2020s, that line has gotten uncannily precise:

  • Twitter/X's 280 characters — trained a "punchline mind": sharp, reduced, extreme;
  • Short video's 15 seconds — rewards a shock opening, and leaves nowhere for depth to live;
  • Comment sections shown in engagement order — the most heated comment always sits on top;
  • Visible like counts — "performative expression" beats "real expression."

When you "speak" on these platforms, you're really playing a game with specific rules. Whatever the rules reward, you gradually learn to say, to feel, to believe.

The deepest impact isn't "you got angry today" — it's that your emotional reaction patterns have been recalibrated by these platforms. It used to take a real event to make you angry — now a headline is enough. It used to take a while to shape an opinion — now you can "output" one in seconds. We used to say "I understand you." Now we say "I'm dissing you."

This is the power of a technological medium rewriting the grammar of feeling — deeper than any advertisement, more lasting than any politics.

✨ Back to the question

So — why does internet product design end up shaping people's emotions, expression, and judgment?

The answer is: because product design doesn't just "carry" information — it decides how information flows, what gets amplified, and what gets suppressed. When billions of people spend hours a day living inside the same "rules for how information moves," those rules end up shaping how they feel the world.

Once you see this, you can ask yourself a question that actually matters:

How much of what I've been feeling lately came from the real world?
And how much of it came from a recommendation algorithm?
