⚖️ Layer 4 · The Senior Engineer's View

Why is tech debt never just a code problem — but something that time, organization, and business pile up together?

Tech debt = the bill for every shortcut the org ever took

🤔 The ordinary user's view: why does this question matter?

"Tech debt" sounds like "the mess programmers leave behind when they write bad code." So a lot of newcomers think — if I write cleanly, follow the conventions, stick to best practices, I won't have any tech debt.

Then you land at a real company and discover it doesn't work that way at all:

  • Old code from five years ago — the person who wrote it left the company long ago;
  • A "temporary" fix from three years back is now on the critical path;
  • A year ago product said "let's ship the big-sale campaign first," and no one ever went back to fix it;
  • Last month the org restructured, and the team that owned this module got broken up.
Tech debt is never just a code problem. It's time (old decisions get older) + organization (people leave, teams dissolve) + business (requirements shift, strategy pivots) — three forces piling up into compound-interest debt.

The key at this layer: tech debt = the bill for every shortcut the org ever took — and someone always has to pay it in the end.

🎯 Peel it back, layer by layer

User layer: only sees the outcome

What users can actually feel about tech debt:

  • Why does this app get slower with every update?
  • Why has this bank's app looked this ugly for ten years?
  • Why was that feature promised a year ago and still isn't shipped?
  • Why does something always break during the big sale?

Users don't say "that's tech debt" — but every degradation in their experience is almost always tech debt quietly collecting interest.

Implementation layer: starting to see the structure

The classic definition comes from Ward Cunningham (1992): "Shipping something less-than-perfect to move fast is like taking out a loan — it runs in the short term, but you'll pay interest long-term."

That definition only covers "code debt." In the real world, tech debt has four sources:

1. Code Debt Cause: rushing, skill gaps, missing review Example: copy-paste, hardcoding, no tests 2. Architecture Debt Cause: early decisions no longer fit today's scale Example: the monolith can't hold up anymore, single-DB single-table is bursting 3. Organization Debt Cause: the author left, teams reshuffled, ownership unclear Example: a critical module nobody dares touch, because no one understands it 4. Knowledge Debt Cause: missing docs, unrecorded decisions, lost context Example: why was it designed this way? Nobody knows anymore

System layer: seeing the full path

The "compound-interest formula" of tech debt looks like this:

Take a shortcut today ↓ Tomorrow's iteration is a bit slower ↓ Six months later, people route around it ↓ A year later, it's legacy ↓ Three years later, nobody dares touch it ↓ Five years later, it gets rewritten

Each step costs 2-3x more to repay than the last. That's "compound interest" — not linear growth, but exponential growth.

Trade-off layer: why was it designed this way?

Healthy tech debt

✅ A conscious choice
✅ A clear repayment plan
✅ Short-term gain > long-term interest
✅ Documented, team knows about it

Example: hardcode to ship for the big sale, then refactor within 2 weeks after

Toxic tech debt

❌ Accumulated unconsciously
❌ No repayment plan
❌ Long-term interest > short-term gain
❌ Nobody knows, nobody owns it

Example: a "temporary fix" from three years ago that's still running on the critical path

Bust the myth: "tech debt = bad" is wrong. Tech debt itself is neutral — even necessary. The question isn't "do you owe debt or not," it's "do you know clearly what you owe, and do you pay it back in time?" Like a credit card — swiping isn't the problem, forgetting to pay is.

🕰️ The time dimension: why yesterday's best practice is today's tech debt

A brutal fact: the code you carefully write today, following "best practices," will probably still get rewritten five years from now.

Why? Because:

  • Business scale grows 10x — the architecture that supported 10K users won't hold 10M;
  • The tech stack moves on — the framework version you use is no longer maintained;
  • Requirements pivot — you started domestic-only, now you need to go global;
  • Teams turn over — the coders have rotated three times.
Time is the strongest amplifier of all tech debt. Debt you don't pay right away will be paid later, in a more expensive form — not because the code got worse, but because the world moved and the code didn't.

🏢 The organization dimension: Conway's Law's hidden aftermath

Remember Conway's Law from Q28? "Architecture grows to look like the org." The reverse is also true: when the org changes, the architecture can't keep up — that's organization debt.

Classic organization-debt scenarios:

  • Team merge: two teams merge, but their systems still speak past each other;
  • Team split: one team splits into two, but nobody wants to inherit that codebase;
  • Attrition: the core author leaves, and the code becomes a "living fossil" — no one dares change it because no one understands it;
  • Business pivot: the company shifts strategy from B2C to B2B, and the whole architecture is built on C-side assumptions.

This kind of debt isn't about badly written code — it's about the org that wrote the code no longer existing.

📈 The business dimension: the compound interest of shifting requirements

Business debt is the hardest to handle, because it's "you did the right thing at the time — the right answer just changed":

V1.0: domestic users only → time as int, Chinese-only storage, single currency → best practice at the time V2.0: go international → timezones become a nightmare, string-encoding bugs explode, multi-currency needs a rewrite → yesterday's right = today's debt V3.0: compliance (GDPR) → user data must be deletable and exportable → the original data model didn't leave room for that → yesterday's right = today's debt V4.0: the AI era → the original data had no tracking, no labels → you add tracking now, but historical data can never be backfilled → yesterday's right = today's debt

💰 When do you pay it back? Three repayment strategies

Big Bang (one-shot rewrite)

✅ Done once, done forever
✅ Architecture gets completely refreshed
❌ Enormous risk
❌ Business grinds to a halt
❌ 90% of rewrite projects fail or slip

Example: Twitter migrating from Rails to JVM

Strangler Fig

✅ Controlled risk
✅ Swap while running
✅ Rollback anytime
❌ Long cycle (1-3 years)
❌ Old-and-new coexistence gets complex

Example: eBay's and Shopify's multi-year refactors

Boy Scout Rule

✅ Every change leaves it a little better
✅ Zero risk, zero cost
✅ Compounds over time
❌ Treats symptoms, not the root
❌ Can't tackle architecture-level debt

Example: everyday code maintenance

Ignore (don't repay, keep carrying it)

✅ No effort short-term
❌ Long-term interest snowballs
❌ Sooner or later it blows up

When it's OK:
the system is about to be retired, the company is dying, or this line of business is about to be shut down

💥 Real case: an archaeology dig into Zhihu's code

Zhihu once shared a "code archaeology" story publicly. In a critical piece of their core search, they found an odd hack — with a comment saying "temporary fix, shipped March 2015."

Turns out that piece of code:

  • The engineer who wrote it left in 2016;
  • The three people who inherited it never dared touch it — they just kept adding code to route around it;
  • By 2020, that hack was affecting 20+ downstream services;
  • It took six months of careful work to safely remove it.
That's the compound-interest effect of tech debt: 5 minutes of shortcut in 2015 → 6 months of repayment in 2020. Interest rate: roughly 10,000x.

✋ Try it yourself

Build your team's "tech-debt ledger"

Grab a piece of paper. Write down all the tech debt your team currently carries:

  1. List every "debt" you can think of (code, architecture, organization, knowledge)
  2. Score each one:
    • Impact scope (1-5)
    • Repayment cost (1-5)
    • Annual interest if not paid (1-5)
  3. Compute priority: impact × annual interest ÷ repayment cost
  4. Pick the Top 3 — those become the debts you must pay next quarter

Do this once, and you're already more mature than 80% of teams — because most teams don't even know how much debt they're carrying.

👑 The Grandmaster's View: how does this shape reality?

Tech debt = the bill for every shortcut the org ever took

The deepest thing about tech debt isn't that it's a "technical problem" — it's that it's the org's mirror in time. Open a company's old codebase, and you're basically flipping through the last 10 years of its history:

See hardcoded values — you know they once rushed a big sale. See awkward architecture — you know someone made a bad strategic bet. See an unowned module — you know somebody left without doing a handoff. See three copies of the same logic — you know there was once an architecture battle that no one won.

Code doesn't lie — it honestly records every choice, every compromise, every failure, every act of forgetting the org has ever made. To truly read a company, don't look at its homepage — its codebase is enough.

The grandmaster's view: tech debt is fundamentally "a bill from past-you to future-you." Every time today you say "let me just note it down and fix it later," you're planting a landmine for some 3 a.m. of your future. The truly mature organizations are the ones willing to pay tomorrow's money today; the truly mature engineers are the ones who believe "future me is also me."

✨ Back to the question

Why is tech debt never just a code problem — but something that time, organization, and business pile up together?

Because code is never something that exists in isolation.

Every line of code was written at a specific time, by a specific person, for a specific business. When the time changes, the person changes, the business pivots — the code becomes a "triple-dislocated-in-time" artifact. It's nobody's fault as an individual — it's the org's time drifting.

So paying off tech debt is never just refactoring code. It's refactoring time (which old decisions should be retired), refactoring the org (who owns what, redrawn), refactoring the business (which historical baggage can be cut) — a three-in-one project.

People who see this layer are the ones who truly cross from "programmer" to "engineer."
