🎯 Layer 1 · The User's View

Why does the platform seem to know exactly what you want to watch?

You never told it what you like — but somehow, it knows

🤔 An ordinary user's view: why does this question matter?

Open TikTok. The first 5 videos are pretty boring. Scroll 10 more — suddenly it's all stuff you actually care about. A week in, the picks feel "creepy-accurate" — you never searched for it, you never liked it, and yet it guessed.

YouTube slides you the next video you didn't know you wanted. NetEase Cloud Music builds a playlist you loop all day. Taobao puts the thing you're about to want right on your homepage — before you even realize you want it.

This isn't luck. It isn't mind-reading either. Behind all of it is a full recommendation system, quietly learning from every swipe, every pause, every skip.

Why does this matter?
More and more of what you watch, buy, listen to, and date is filtered by algorithms first. Understanding how they work is understanding why the "world" you see looks different from the one your friend sees.

🧩 Break the big question into small ones

  1. How does the platform figure out what you like? Which of your behaviors does it read as preference?
  2. Why does it get "better and better" at guessing? How does it actually "learn"?
  3. Does it push what you truly like — or just what you'll click? These are not the same thing.
  4. Why do two people open the same app and see completely different worlds?

🎯 Peel it back, layer by layer

The user's view: only the outcome

You only feel two things — see something you like, feel good; see something you don't, swipe away fast.

But here's what you don't see: the swipe itself is the strongest signal. Every millisecond you spend on a video is being recorded. 3-second stop vs 10-second stop vs full watch vs like vs share — each is a vote, and together they decide what shows up next.

The build layer: starting to see the structure

The classic approach is called collaborative filtering — find people whose behavior looks like yours, see what they like, and you'll probably like it too.

🧍 You watched A, B, C
↓ Find someone "very much like you"
🧍 They watched A, B, C — and also D
↓ Inference
🎯 So D goes to you too

The more modern move is to turn each user and each piece of content into a string of numbers (called an embedding vector), and then use "how close are the two strings" to measure "how likely are you to like it."

# Minimal pseudocode: how one recommendation gets computed user_vec = embed_user(user_id) # [0.12, -0.34, 0.88, ...] 256 dims candidates = recall(user_vec, top=5000) # coarse filter from tens of millions down to 5000 # Ranking: score each candidate with "probability you'll like it" scores = [] for item in candidates: item_vec = embed_item(item.id) p_click = model_ctr(user_vec, item_vec, context) # will you click? p_finish = model_finish(user_vec, item_vec) # will you finish it? p_like = model_like(user_vec, item_vec) # will you like it? # Multi-objective blend: different products weight these differently score = 0.4 * p_click + 0.3 * p_finish + 0.3 * p_like scores.append((item, score)) feed = sorted(scores, key=lambda x: -x[1])[:20] # the 20 items your pull-to-refresh returns

The system layer: seeing the full path

From "you flick your thumb" to "the next video is pushed to you" is actually a giant pipeline running millions of times per second:

📱 Every action you take
↓ Log ingestion
📊 Behavior log system
↓ Feature engineering
🧠 Model training (offline + real-time)
↓ Deploy
🎯 Recommendation service: recall → coarse ranking → fine ranking → re-ranking
↓ Return
📱 Your next screen
There's a hidden weapon in this whole path: A/B testing. A platform runs dozens or hundreds of "experiment versions" at the same time — 1% of users get bucket A, another 1% get bucket B, and it watches which one has higher completion rate, better retention. The winner scales. That's the real engine behind "it keeps getting better at knowing you" — not one genius algorithm delivered in one shot, but thousands of tiny victories per day, compounding.

The trade-off view: why is it designed this way?

Here's the question that keeps engineers and PMs up at night: "what you like" and "what you'll click" are not the same thing.

Optimize for click-through rate

Short-term metrics rocket. You keep scrolling. But content drifts toward clickbait, outrage, extremes — because those are the things that "trick you into tapping" the best.

Optimize for long-term satisfaction

Sounds right — but hard to measure. What even counts as "satisfied"? Retention? Return visits? Or "will you still recommend this to a friend six months from now"? Weak signal, slow training.

So every platform is quietly trading off: one more second now vs a return in five years — novelty vs familiarity — pushing viral hits vs pushing niche quality. Every parameter in a recommendation system is a values choice — just one hidden inside engineers' spreadsheets and A/B win rates.

There's also a side effect you can't ignore: the filter bubble. The better it knows you, the more it leans toward "things you'll like" — and the less you see of "things you won't like but might matter." Over time, you think "the world just is this way" — but really, it's "the world as the algorithm thinks you see it."

✋ Try it yourself

Try this — open a "burner" account for yourself

Sign up for a brand-new TikTok / YouTube account. For the first 30 minutes, watch only one kind of content (say, only cats). Then scroll freely for another 10 minutes, and watch how the mix of what it pushes shifts.

Now do it again with a different identity — this time only watch something completely different (say, cooking). Compare the two worlds. You'll see, with your own eyes, how "you, in the eyes of the algorithm" gets shaped.

👑 The Grandmaster's View: how does this shape reality?

From tech to world

We used to think "taste is something you grow on your own" — the songs you love, the movies you rewatch, the team you cheer for — all free choices, all yours. More and more evidence says otherwise: half of your taste is being shaped by the recommendation system. It guesses what you'll like — you get pushed it enough times — and eventually you really do like it — and then that becomes a positive training example the next round.

This is a very quiet kind of power. It doesn't tell you what you can't see — it just doesn't show you certain things. It doesn't tell you what to believe — it just makes some views appear three times a day, and others never at all.

What's more unsettling: the optimize target of these systems is their business goal, not your life goal. It wants you to scroll three more minutes — not to make you happier, wiser, or braver. Sometimes those overlap. Often they don't.

So understanding recommendation systems is really understanding one thing: the feed in front of you isn't "the world" — it's "a version of the world customized for you". And the one doing the customizing — that's not you.

✨ Back to the question

Why does the platform seem to know exactly what you want to watch?

The answer: because every swipe, every pause, every like, every skip of yours gets turned into a number, and fed into a model that updates itself every single day. It doesn't "know you" — it uses your past behavior to predict your next second of behavior, and then pushes whichever prediction it's most confident about right in front of your eyes. Over time, what it shapes isn't just your feed — it's your taste, your mood, the self you think you are.
