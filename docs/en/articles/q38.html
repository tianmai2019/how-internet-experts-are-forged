👑 Layer 5 · The Grandmaster's View

Why do business goals so often decide the technology roadmap — and not the other way around?

Technology isn't a tree that grows on its own — it's the child of a business

🤔 An ordinary user's view: why does this question matter?

Look at social products —

  • Facebook spent 10 years rebuilding itself around "mobile-first" — because ad money moved to phones;
  • WeChat has always been "restrained" — because its business is messaging plus ecosystem, not raw traffic;
  • TikTok pours money into its recommendation algorithm — because its business is "attention for ads";
  • Slack pours money into stability — because it sells B2B subscriptions, and downtime means refunds.

Have you ever wondered: why are their technical choices so different?

Because technology isn't the "optimal solution" — technology is "the child of the business model."

How you get paid decides which metric you must serve;
The metric you must serve decides which technology you must optimize;
The technology you must optimize decides your architecture, your language, your algorithms, your org chart.

🎯 Peel it back, layer by layer

🎯 The User's View: only sees the result

The user experiences: "This app has so many ads." "This app is free but the recommendations are eerily accurate." "This app charges a monthly fee but it's rock-solid." None of this is coincidence — it's the business model projected directly onto user experience.

🔨 The Beginner Coder's View: starting to see the wiring

The mapping from business goal → key metric → technical investment:

// Ad-driven products (Facebook / TikTok / Weibo) Key Metric: DAU × Time Spent × Ad Impressions × CPM Tech Focus: - Recommendation algorithms (maximize attention) - Video codecs (cut bandwidth cost) - Ad-serving systems (millisecond bidding) - A/B test infrastructure (hundreds of experiments a day) // Subscription-driven products (Netflix / Slack / Notion) Key Metric: Paying Users × ARPU × Retention Tech Focus: - Stability (downtime = refunds) - Content quality (Netflix even builds its own CDN) - Enterprise-grade security (SOC2, SSO, audit logs) - Customer success systems (not "growth hacking") // Transaction-driven products (Taobao / Pinduoduo / Meituan) Key Metric: GMV × Take Rate Tech Focus: - Search/recommendation (match products to people) - Inventory/logistics/payment (highly consistent distributed systems) - Anti-fraud/risk control (protect every cent) - Peak-day capacity (Double 11 needs 100× normal capacity)

See it? They're all called "backend engineers" — but the actual work in these three kinds of companies is completely different. Their technical skills are ultimately shaped by how the company makes money.

🌊 The Engineer's View: seeing the full path

Facebook's 2012 "mobile rebuild" is a textbook case:

2011: 70% of ad revenue comes from desktop web ↓ But 60%+ of user time is on phones ↓ Business pressure: if we don't go mobile, ad revenue falls off a cliff ↓ Facebook spends over a year killing the HTML5 hybrid approach ↓ Rewrites iOS/Android as native apps ↓ Rebuilds the ad-serving system for a mobile feed ↓ 2014: mobile ad revenue overtakes desktop ↓ Zuckerberg later said: "This was the most important technical decision in the company's history."

That decision wasn't driven by "the tech is more advanced" — it was driven by the survival of the business model.

⚖️ The Senior Engineer's View: why design it this way?

The correspondence between business model and technology roadmap can be read as a "trade-off matrix":

Free + ad model

Tech must serve "attention maximization" — recommendations, algorithms, A/B testing. User experience gets held hostage by "time spent."

Paid subscription model

Tech must serve "long-term user value" — stability, privacy, experience. User experience is the product.

Transaction / commission model

Tech must serve "every transaction succeeds" — consistency, risk control, fulfillment. The tech has to count every penny.

Open-source / developer model

Tech must serve "a thriving ecosystem" — docs, stable APIs, community. The tech itself is the product.

"Tech stack choice" looks like an engineering call — it's really a business call. That's why real technical leaders sit next to the CEO — not tucked away in a server room.

👑 The Grandmaster's View: how does this shape reality?

The technology roadmap is the child of the business model

There's a myth in tech circles: "I just do tech, I don't ask about the business." But what the grandmaster's view reveals is this: which technology you pick, which metric you optimize, how late you stay at the office — the business model is making all of those decisions for you.

It explains a lot of "the fate of the tech industry":

  • Why do algorithm engineers get paid the most? Because they sit directly on the "recommendation → click → ad" money path;
  • Why does ops have such low status at many internet companies? Because their value is "fewer crashes" — and crashes are rare;
  • Why is a company like Cloudflare willing to pay a fortune for a security team? Because what they sell is "never going down";
  • Why did GitHub change so much once it went from "personal tool" to "enterprise SaaS"? — Because the person paying the bill changed.

Push it further: the technical direction of the entire big-tech industry is, at bottom, defined by capital markets and revenue mix. Ad-revenue companies will always build recommendations. Subscription-revenue companies will always build stability. Transaction-revenue companies will always build risk control. If you want tech to "take a different path," first change where its money comes from.

So "technical autonomy" is a beautiful but fragile slogan. Real technical autonomy is knowing whose money you're writing code for — and having the power to choose.

✨ Back to the question

So — why do business goals so often decide the technology roadmap?

The answer: because technology needs resources, resources need funding, and funding needs a business model to sustain it. This chain doesn't start from "what technology can do" — it starts from "what users are willing to pay for." Technology isn't a tree growing "top-down" — it's a plant growing "up from the money." Your business model is its soil.

The grandmaster's awakening:

Next time you hear "this is a technical decision," take one more step —
whose money is driving this decision? What do they want?
Once you see that layer, you're actually starting to understand what an internet company is.
