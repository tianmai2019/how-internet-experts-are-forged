🌟 Closing question

Why does deeper technical understanding ultimately lead to understanding people, organizations, and society?

At the end of technology — you find human nature.

🎬 A real observation

Have you ever noticed something?

Top engineers, more often than not, don't talk about "technology."

They talk about:
- how teams collaborate
- how decisions get made
- how organizations evolve
- why users think the way they do
- what the social trends are...

Why? Have they "changed"?

No. They've just noticed something: technical problems, taken far enough, all turn into human problems.

🌱 A technologist's journey of understanding

An engineer's growth usually follows this path:

Junior: cares about "syntax" How do I write this for-loop? How do I call this API?
Mid-level: cares about "design" How do I organize this code? How do I split this module?
Senior: cares about "architecture" How does the whole system evolve? How does it handle future change?
Expert: cares about "collaboration" How do multiple teams coordinate? How do decisions get made?
Top-tier: cares about "impact" How does this technology affect users? How does it shape behavior? How does it change society?

You'll notice: the higher you go, the closer you get to things "beyond technology."

🤔 Why does this happen?

1. Because technology is made by people

Code doesn't fall from the sky — it's written by people.

So you'll notice:

  • Code quality reflects the engineer's skill
  • System architecture reflects the team's capability
  • Technology choices reflect the company's culture
  • Release cadence reflects the organization's maturity

To understand the code, you have to understand the people who write it.

2. Because technology reflects the organization

Conway's Law

"Organizations that design systems... are constrained to produce designs which are
copies of their communication structures."

— Melvin Conway, 1967

In plain English:

A system built by 3 teams tends to end up with 3 modules.

The interfaces between modules mirror the communication interfaces between teams.
If two teams don't get along, the modules they build won't play nicely either.

That's why the software architecture of a big company usually mirrors its org chart.

3. Because technology serves people

Technology doesn't exist for its own sake — it exists to solve human problems.

So:

  • To build a good recommender, you have to understand human attention and psychology
  • To build a good search engine, you have to understand how people express and seek
  • To build a good social product, you have to understand human social needs
  • To build anything good, you have to understand what people want

4. Because technology reshapes society

When technology gets big enough, it starts affecting society.

And then you can't help but ask:

- What consequences will this technology have?
- Who benefits? Who gets hurt?
- Does it need to be regulated?
- Does it match our values?

None of these are "technical questions" anymore.

🌍 The world beyond technology

So truly going deep into technology eventually takes you here:

  • Psychology: why do people react this way?
  • Sociology: what are the rules of group behavior?
  • Economics: how do incentives shape behavior?
  • Organizational theory: how do people coordinate?
  • Ethics: what's right? what's wrong?
  • History: have these patterns happened before?
  • Philosophy: what does all of this mean?

That's why real technology grandmasters tend to be "generalists" — not "specialists."

"Take technology far enough, and it leads to human nature."

👑 The Grandmaster's View: technology is just the doorway

People who really understand technology end up understanding the world

Technology is a door to the world:

  • You start with code
  • Then you see systems
  • Then you see people
  • Then you see organizations
  • Then you see society
  • And finally — you see the world itself.

That's what the phrase "internet grandmaster" really means:
not "someone who understands internet technology,"
but "someone who, through the internet, has come to see the whole world."

✨ Back to the question

So — why does deeper technical understanding ultimately lead to understanding people, organizations, and society?

Because technology never exists on its own.

It's made by people, serves people, is realized through organizations, and ultimately affects society.

When you really go into the core of technology, you'll find — you're actually going into the core of human nature.

That's why the greatest technology grandmasters
are often the deepest humanists.
